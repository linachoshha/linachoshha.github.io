How brain-inspired AI could help us understand the mind (and not just build smarter machines).

We often talk about making AI more intelligent.
But what if intelligence isn’t the goal?
What if the real frontier is understanding how we make meaning? how the brain turns words, stories, and experiences into understanding?

My mission (or maybe my passion) has always been to understand.
To zoom in: into the human mind, the body, the environment, and the invisible processes that connect them.

I’m not fascinated by building ever more intelligent machines that we can’t understand.
I’m fascinated by using intelligent systems to make us more intelligent, to understand ourselves better. Human-centred AI.

🧠 Can LLMs act as a cognitive lab?

What if Large Language Models could help us study how minds make meaning?
What if we could build brain-based adapters — models that don’t just predict the next word, but reflect how our brains interpret stories?

Recent work like BrainLLM (Ye et al., 2023) shows that LLMs can align with neural signals (fMRI) to reconstruct or predict language from brain activity.
But what if we reversed it by using brain-inspired adapters to understand how meaning forms in us?

✏️ Example

Prompt:

Tonight I went to sleep at 2 a.m.
Suddenly I woke up to a loud noise. At first I thought it was my alarm clock, but it turned out to be the fire alarm.
I couldn’t tell whether it came from my room or from the whole building.
With one eye open, I grabbed my hijab and coat and ran downstairs.

Original continution of this scene was:

By 5 a.m., we were all outside, wrapped in robes and pajamas, hair wild, eyes half-closed, standing in the cold. That was how I met my neighbours for the first time.

How would a “plain” LLM have continued this prompt?

Plain LLM continuation:

People were standing in the hallway looking confused.
Someone said it was a false alarm, and we all went back to sleep.

Now, let’s assume we have an fMRI recording from someone who read this scene. We build a model, which we train with prompt + fMRI of the (original) continuation. How such brain-adapter LLM will continue the prompt?

🧩 Brain-adapter LLM continuation:

We stood outside in the freezing air, half-awake and sockless.

🎯 Takeaway

The plain LLM follows linguistic probability — it closes the event quickly.
The brain-adapted LLM follows human experience — it lingers where perception and meaning form: the cold air, the group, the small shared humor. A small but measurable tilt toward real human continuation.

These examples illustrate the kind of differences we might observe if such brain-adapted models were trained and tested in practice.

✏️ Another example

Prompt:

I used to spot our cat snoozing in a basket high on the shelf; I found it odd, since he usually preferred the sunny windowsill or the couch.
One morning, I finally climbed up to see what he was doing over there that captivated him.
There he lay, his furry belly overflowing the basket’s edges, eyes half-closed in contentment.

Original continuation:

When I leaned closer, I caught a faint but familiar wool scent: my mother’s knitted pink sweater was bunched at the bottom, right under his paws.

Plain LLM continuation:

I figured he just liked the quiet spot where no one would bother him.
Maybe the basket felt safe.

🧩 Brain-adapted continuation:

The cat didn’t stir, just twitched an ear, as if he already knew I was there.

(Keeps the same neural “shape” : recognition, calm warmth, sensory realism without naming mother or scent. Smooth, minimal, human-like: the subtle hippocampal-vmPFC tone translated into language.)

💡 Why this matters

If we can model how meaning emerges, not just how text unfolds,
we can begin to see how stories shape minds (and how minds shape stories).

That’s the vision behind Narrative Mirror:
A cognitive-interpretability framework inspired by BrainLLM,
translating brain, behavior, and Bayesian reasoning into LLM semantics.

It’s not about teaching machines to think like us.
It’s about using machines to understand how we think.
